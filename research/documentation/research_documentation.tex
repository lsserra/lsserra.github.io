\subsection{Relative Vector Geometry: Camera and Rigid Body}
\label{sec:geometry-3d}

To transform points on a rigid body frame $B$ to points in a camera frame $C$, we can express the operation using both a 3D affine vector notation and a $4 \times 4$ homogeneous transformation matrix.

\subsubsection{Frame Definitions}

\begin{equation}
   \mathit{C} = \{ \mathbf{C}, \hat{\vect{c}}_1, \hat{\vect{c}}_2, \hat{\vect{c}}_3 \}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
   \mathit{B} = \{ \mathbf{B}, \hat{\vect{b}}_1, \hat{\vect{b}}_2, \hat{\vect{b}}_3 \}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Measurement Point Cloud Point Definition}
The point cloud derived from measurement source has $P$ number of points, which mathematically are vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ of each point wrt the camera origin, $\mathbf{C}$, expressed in the $\mathit{C}$ frame.

\begin{equation}
\pCiC , \quad i = 1, 2, 3,\dots, P
\end{equation}

    \caption{Measurement-derived point cloud, expressed in the camera frame $C$.}
    \label{fig:pcd-meas-only}
\end{figure}


\pagebreak


\subsubsection{CAD Model Point Cloud Points Definition}
The point cloud derived from the CAD model may not have the same number of points as the measurement itself. The CAD model point cloud has $Q$ number of points, which mathematically are vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ of each point wrt the CAD PCD origin, $\mathbf{B}$, expressed in the $\mathit{B}$ frame.

\begin{equation}
\pBjB , \quad j = 1, 2, 3,\dots, Q
\end{equation}

\begin{figure}[thbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{../../presentations/panel_interview/images/research/diags/PCD_CAD_GEOM.png}
    \caption{CAD-model point cloud, expressed in the rigid-body frame $B$.}
    \label{fig:pcd-cad-geom}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{The Rigid-Body Transformation in $\mathbb{R}^3$}
The standard 3D kinematic translation and rotation mapping is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \pCiC = \TCB \pBjB + \pBCC
    \label{eq:pcd-measurement-model}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Generic Homogeneous Transformation in $\mathbb{R}^4$}
By appending a scaling factor of 1 to our coordinate vectors, the rigid body mapping can be cleanly expressed as a single $4 \times 4$ matrix multiplication:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \pCiC \\
        1
    \end{bmatrix}
    = \TBCtoC
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \pBiB \\
        1
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Where the homogeneous transformation matrix $\TBCtoC$ is explicitly partitioned as:
\begin{equation}
    \TBCtoC =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \TCB & \pBCC \\
        \vect{0}_{1\times3} & 1
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

Expanding this reveals the complete linear algebraic formulation:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \pCiC \\
        1
    \end{bmatrix}
    =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        & & &  \\
        & \TCB & & \pBCC \\
        & & &  \\
        &\vect{0}^T & & 1
    \end{bmatrix}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \pBiB \\
        1
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Pinhole Camera}
\label{sec:pinhole-camera}

The geometry of Section~\ref{sec:geometry-3d} describes points in $\mathbb{R}^3$. To relate that geometry to what is actually observed by a camera, we insert the image plane: a 3D point in the camera frame is projected through the pinhole camera model onto a 2D pixel-coordinate measurement.

\paragraph{Perspective (Pinhole) Projection.}
A 3D point expressed in the camera frame, $\pCiC$, is projected onto the normalized image plane by dividing through by its depth (third component), $Z_j$:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \tilde{x}_i \\
        \tilde{y}_i \\
        1
    \end{bmatrix}
    = \tilde{\vect{p}}^{\,C}_{p_i/C}
    = \frac{1}{Z_j}\left\{ \pCiC \right\}
    = \frac{1}{Z_j}\left\{ \TCB \pBjB + \pBCC \right\}
    \label{eq:pinhole-projection}
\end{equation}

This is the perspective projection of the 3D rigid-body geometry from Eq.~\eqref{eq:pcd-measurement-model}.

The image has width $W$ and height $H$ (in pixels), with pixel coordinates $(u,v)$ measured from the top-left corner. The normalized image-plane coordinates are mapped into pixel coordinates by the camera intrinsic matrix $\Kmat$:

\begin{equation}
    \Kmat =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        f_x & 0 & c_x \\
        0 & f_y & c_y \\
        0 & 0 & 1
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}

where $f$ is the focal length (expressed in pixels) and $(c_x, c_y)$ is the principal point. The resulting pixel-space measurement is:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x_{p_i/C} \\
        y_{p_i/C} \\
        1
    \end{bmatrix}
    = \bar{\vect{p}}^{\,C}_{p_i/C}
    = \Kmat \, \tilde{\vect{p}}^{\,C}_{p_i/C}
    \label{eq:pixel-measurement}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Focal Length from Horizontal Field of View.}

Given the horizontal field of view $\theta_H$ and image width $W$, the focal length (in pixels) can be recovered from the pinhole geometry:

\begin{equation}
    \tan\left( \frac{\theta_H}{2} \right) = \frac{W}{2f}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    f = \frac{W}{2}\left( \tan \frac{\theta_H}{2} \right)^{-1} \quad \text{[pixels]}
\end{equation}

For example, for an image resolution of $W \times H = 672 \times 376$ pixels, this relation gives the focal length in pixels directly from the sensor's horizontal FOV.

\section{Optimal Parameter Estimation}
\label{sec:optimal-parameter-estimation}

Two complementary families of parameter-estimation technique are used throughout this document: \emph{linear} least-squares estimation, for measurement models that are already linear in the unknown parameters (Section~\ref{sec:linear-lse}); and \emph{nonlinear} least-squares estimation, for measurement models that must first be linearized about a reference operating point and then solved iteratively (Section~\ref{sec:nlls-estimation}).

\subsection{Linear Least Squared Error Estimation}
\label{sec:linear-lse}

The bilinear absolute-orientation problem of Section~\ref{sec:closed-form} reduces, after a change of variables, to a measurement model that is linear in the unknown parameters. The general form of that linear system, and its least-squares solution, is given below --- copied verbatim from the author's notes.

\begin{authornotes}{9/17/26 (cont.)}
\begin{equation}
    \vect{y} = \begin{bmatrix} -\skewop{\left( \pCiC + \pBjB \right)} & \mat{I}_{3\times 3} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \qCB \\ \bvec \end{bmatrix} = \mat{H}\vect{x} \qquad (3\times 6) \ (6 \times 1)
\end{equation}

Now this is a data fitting problem that we choose to solve with the minimum squared error technique.

\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\vect{y}} = \begin{bmatrix} \vect{y}_1 \\ \vdots \\ \vect{y}_P \end{bmatrix} \, (3P \times 1) \, , \qquad \tilde{\mat{H}} = \begin{bmatrix} \mat{H}_1 \\ \vdots \\ \mat{H}_P \end{bmatrix} \, (3P \times 6)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \Rightarrow \hat{\vect{x}} = \left( \tilde{\mat{H}}^T \tilde{\mat{H}} \right)^{-1} \tilde{\mat{H}}^T \tilde{\vect{y}} \qquad (6 \times 6 \text{ inversion})
    \label{eq:linear-lse-solution}
\end{equation}

*How many $P$ observations you need is directly derived from the ability to invert $\left( \tilde{\mat{H}}^T \tilde{\mat{H}} \right)$; it is dependent on the rank of $\left( \tilde{\mat{H}}^T \tilde{\mat{H}} \right)$.
\end{authornotes}

Eq.~\eqref{eq:linear-lse-solution} is the general linear least-squares result invoked throughout this document: both the 6-DOF bilinear solve and the 3-DOF centroid-differenced OLAE solve of Section~\ref{sec:closed-form} are applications of it, with $\mat{H}_i$ and $\vect{y}_i$ specialized to each case.

\subsection{Non-Linear Least Squared Error Estimation}
\label{sec:nlls-estimation}

With the geometry of Section~\ref{sec:geometry-3d} (and, by extension, Section~\ref{sec:pinhole-camera}) established, it can now be used to estimate the unknown parameters of interest by nonlinear least squares. The \emph{state} is defined to be the specific set of parameters we wish to estimate --- here, the relative pose of the camera with respect to the rigid body:

\begin{equation}
    \stateVec = \begin{bmatrix} \pBCC \\ \rotErr \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^n
\end{equation}

where $\pBCC$ is the relative position and $\rotErr$ is an attitude correction parameterizing $\TCB$ relative to a reference (linearization-point) attitude estimate $B'$. (A closed-form alternative that avoids linearizing the attitude altogether is given in Section~\ref{sec:closed-form}, using the linear estimator of Section~\ref{sec:linear-lse} directly.)

To estimate $\stateVec$, we must: define a cost function; linearize the (nonlinear) measurement model; choose which states to estimate; and iteratively update the state estimate to minimize the cost.

\subsubsection{Defining the Cost Function}

\paragraph{Generic Least-Squares Error (LSE) Cost Function.}

In general, given a measurement $\vect{z}$ and a measurement model $\vect{h}(\stateVec)$ that predicts that measurement from the current state estimate, the weighted least-squares cost function is:

\begin{equation}
    J = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{P} \err_i^T \err_i \, , \qquad
    \err_i = \vect{z}_i - \vect{h}(\stateVec)
\end{equation}

The specific form of the measurement $\vect{z}_i$ and the measurement model $\vect{h}(\stateVec)$ depend on which sensing modality is used, as detailed below.

\paragraph{Applying the LSE Cost Function to Point Cloud Measurements.}

\textbf{Measurement:} The point cloud measurement is $\pCiC$, the $i$-th measured point expressed in the camera frame $C$ (Section~\ref{sec:geometry-3d}).

\textbf{Measurement model:} The model predicts $\pCiC$ from the current state estimate using the rigid-body transformation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pcd-measurement-model}:

\begin{equation}
    \vect{h}(\stateVec) = \mat{T}^{C}_{B'} \left[ \mat{I}_3 - \skewop{\rotErr} \right] \pBjB + \pBCC
\end{equation}

\textbf{Error:}
\begin{equation}
    \err_i = \pCiC - \vect{h}(\stateVec)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Point-to-Plane Variant.} Rather than minimizing the raw 3D residual above (point-to-point), the error can instead be projected onto the unit surface normal $\nCiC$ at the target point, which penalizes only the component of the residual normal to the target surface:
\begin{equation}
    \err_i = \left[ \pCiC - \vect{h}(\stateVec) \right] \cdot \nCiC
\end{equation}
so that the corresponding point-to-plane cost function is:
\begin{equation}
    J = \sum_{\corrSet} \left[ \left( \pCiC - (\TCB \pBjB + \pBCC) \right) \cdot \nCiC \right]^2
\end{equation}
Expressed compactly in homogeneous coordinates ($\mathbb{R}^4$), let $\tilde{\vect{p}}$ denote the homogeneous vector $[ \vect{p}^T, 1 ]^T$. The point-to-plane cost function is then:
\begin{equation}
    E(\TBCtoC) = \sum_{i} \left[ \left( \mathbf{\Pi} \TBCtoC \tilde{\vect{p}}^B_{q_{i}/B} - \pCiC \right)^T \nCiC \right]^2
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{\Pi} = \begin{bmatrix} \mat{I}_{3\times3} & \vect{0}_{3\times1} \end{bmatrix}$ is a projection matrix used to drop the homogeneous scaling factor back down to $\mathbb{R}^3$ for the dot product calculation.

\paragraph{Applying the LSE Cost Function to Image Measurements.}

\textbf{Measurement:} The image measurement is $\bar{\vect{p}}^{\,C}_{p_i/C}$, the $i$-th observed pixel coordinate (Section~\ref{sec:pinhole-camera}).

\textbf{Measurement model:} The model predicts the pixel measurement by composing the rigid-body transform with the pinhole projection of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pixel-measurement}:

\begin{equation}
    \vect{h}(\stateVec) = \Kmat \, \frac{1}{Z}\left\{ \mat{T}^{C}_{B'} \left[ \mat{I}_3 - \skewop{\rotErr} \right] \pBjB + \pBCC \right\}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Error:}
\begin{equation}
    \err_i = \bar{\vect{p}}^{\,C}_{p_i/C} - \vect{h}(\stateVec)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{How to Solve the Nonlinear Least Squares (NLLS) Problem}

Once a measurement, a measurement model, and a correspondence set (Section~\ref{sec:correspondence-problem}) have been chosen, we must still choose \emph{how} to solve the resulting NLLS problem.

\paragraph{Method 1: Gauss-Newton.}

Because $\vect{h}(\stateVec)$ is nonlinear in $\stateVec$ (for either the point cloud or the image measurement model above), it is linearized about an operating point $\stateVec^*$ via a Taylor series expansion:

\begin{equation}
    \vect{h}(\stateVec) \approx \vect{h}(\stateVec^*) + \Jac\Big|_{\stateVec^*} (\stateVec - \stateVec^*) + (\stateVec - \stateVec^*)^T \mat{H}_{xx}\Big|_{\stateVec^*} (\stateVec - \stateVec^*) + \text{H.O.T.}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \dstate = \stateVec - \stateVec^*
\end{equation}

where the Jacobian of $\vect{h}$ with respect to the state (computed from whichever measurement model was selected above) is:

\begin{equation}
    \Jac =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \dfrac{\partial h_1}{\partial x_1} & \dfrac{\partial h_1}{\partial x_2} & \cdots & \dfrac{\partial h_1}{\partial x_n} \\[2mm]
        \vdots & & & \vdots \\[1mm]
        \dfrac{\partial h_m}{\partial x_1} & \cdots & & \dfrac{\partial h_m}{\partial x_n}
    \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}
\end{equation}

Dropping the higher-order terms, the linearized residual for the $i$-th measurement is:

\begin{equation}
    \err_i = \measBar_i - \left\{ \vect{h}(\stateVec^*) + \Jac\Big|_{\stateVec^*} \dstate \right\} \triangleq \measTilde_i
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Concatenated (Stacked) Least-Squares System.} Stacking all $P$ measurements, the cost function can be written compactly as:

\begin{equation}
    J = \frac{1}{2} \left( \stackMeas - \stackJac \, \dstate \right)^T \left( \stackMeas - \stackJac \, \dstate \right)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \stackMeas =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \measTilde_1 \\
        \vdots \\
        \measTilde_P
    \end{bmatrix}
    \, , \qquad
    \stackJac =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \Jac\big|_{\stateVec^*_1} \\
        \vdots \\
        \Jac\big|_{\stateVec^*_P}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \in \mathbb{R}^{(P \cdot m) \times n}
\end{equation}

where
\begin{align}
    P &= \text{number of measurements} \\
    m &= \text{size of each measurement} \\
    n &= \text{size of the state}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Normal Equations.} The correction $\dstate$ that minimizes $J$ is found by setting $\partial J / \partial \dstate = 0$ --- this is precisely the linear least-squares problem of Section~\ref{sec:linear-lse}, with $\mat{H}\to\stackJac$, $\vect{y}\to\stackMeas$, and $\vect{x}\to\dstate$:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial J}{\partial \dstate} = 0 = \left( -\stackJac \right)^T \left( \stackMeas - \stackJac \, \dstate \right)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \left( \stackJac^T \stackJac \right) \dstate = \stackJac^T \stackMeas
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \dstateHat = \left( \stackJac^T \stackJac \right)^{-1} \left( \stackJac^T \stackMeas \right)
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Iterative Update.} The solution is refined iteratively:

\begin{itemize}
    \item If $\| \dstateHat \| < \epsilon$: the estimate has \textbf{converged}; terminate the iteration.
    \item Otherwise: update the state estimate,
    \begin{equation}
        \stateVec = \stateVec^* + \dstateHat
    \end{equation}
    recompute the Jacobians $\Jac$ at the new linearization point, and re-solve for another $\dstateHat$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Closed-Form Solutions to the Absolute Orientation Problem}
\label{sec:closed-form}

The measurement model of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pcd-measurement-model} can be solved for $\TCB$ and $\pBCC$ directly, in closed form, rather than by linearizing about a reference attitude and iterating (Section~\ref{sec:nlls-estimation}). This is the classical \emph{absolute orientation} problem: given a set of true correspondences between two point sets, recover the rigid-body transformation that best aligns them. The derivation below is copied verbatim from the author's own notes and proceeds in two stages: a general bilinear formulation that solves for the full 6-DOF transformation ($\TCB$ and $\pBCC$ jointly), and a reduction to a 3-DOF attitude-only problem obtained by centroid-differencing the two point sets, solved via the Optimal Linear Attitude Estimator (OLAE). Both stages reduce to the linear least-squares result of Section~\ref{sec:linear-lse}.

\subsection{The Bilinear Formulation (6 DOF)}

\begin{authornotes}{9/17/26}
Let's develop some mathematical rigor today.

\section{The Correspondence Problem}
\label{sec:correspondence-problem}

Each measurement $\vect{z}_i$ has an associated CAD model point $\pBjB$ that the true $\TCB, \pBCC$ would map exactly onto $\vect{z}_i$ in a noiseless setting. This pairing is not known \emph{a priori} and must be established:
\begin{equation}
\corrSet = \{ (p_{i},q_{j}) \}
\end{equation}
Even with noise-free measurements, $p_{i}$ and $q_{j}$ need not be the same physical point, since the CAD model is only a discrete sampling of the true surface.

Correspondences are established --- from either point cloud or monocular (image) measurements --- through some combination of feature descriptor matching, nearest-neighbor search, reprojection-based matching, or robust random-sample consensus (RANSAC, Section~\ref{sec:ransac-absolute-orientation}).

\paragraph{Point Cloud Measurements.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nearest-neighbor matching}: associate each measured point $\pCiC$ with the closest CAD point $\pBjB$ under the current transform estimate (as in Iterative Closest Point, ICP).
    \item \textbf{Local geometric feature descriptors}: match points using descriptors of local surface geometry (e.g.\ curvature, normals, FPFH), more robust to a poor initial alignment than raw nearest-neighbor search.
    \item \textbf{RANSAC-based initial alignment}: a robust random-sampling scheme over candidate correspondences, used to obtain a coarse initial transform (Section~\ref{sec:ransac-absolute-orientation}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Monocular (Image) Measurements.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Keypoint detection and descriptor matching}: detect and match 2D keypoints (e.g.\ ORB, SIFT) between the image and a rendered or previously observed view of the CAD model.
    \item \textbf{Reprojection-based matching}: project the CAD model points into the image using the current pose estimate and associate each with the nearest detected image feature.
    \item \textbf{Robust outlier rejection}: RANSAC (e.g.\ within a PnP solve), used to reject incorrect correspondences (Section~\ref{sec:ransac-absolute-orientation}).
    \item \textbf{Moving-Edges Search with Normal-Direction Gating}: 1D search along the surface normal at each synthetic (projected CAD) edge point; select candidate points by minimum reprojected pixel distance; gate on real/synthetic gradient-direction difference; establish 2D-2D correspondences between the projected CAD edges and the observed image edges:
\begin{center}
\begin{math}
\corrSet = \{ (p_{i},q_{j}) \} \rightarrow \corrSet = \{ (\bar{\vect{p}}^{\,C}_{p_i/C},\pBjB) \}
\end{math}
\end{center}
\end{itemize}

Whichever method is used above, the result is only a \emph{candidate} correspondence set --- it will generally contain mismatches and outliers. RANSAC is what finds the \textbf{consensus set}: the largest inlier subset of a candidate correspondence set that is consistent with a single rigid-body transformation. RANSAC does not generate correspondences on its own; an initial correspondence set must therefore already be established, by one of the methods above, in order to seed it.

\subsection{RANSAC}
\label{sec:ransac-absolute-orientation}

At its core, each RANSAC iteration solves a parameter estimation problem: it draws a minimal correspondence subset and solves for the rigid-body transformation via the closed-form OLAE result of Section~\ref{sec:closed-form} (itself an application of the linear least-squares result of Section~\ref{sec:linear-lse}). RANSAC must be seeded with an initial correspondence set (Section~\ref{sec:correspondence-problem}) --- it refines a correspondence set, it does not create one from raw measurements. The output of RANSAC is the \textbf{consensus set}, together with the parameter estimate obtained over it. The author's notes below lay out this connection explicitly.

\begin{authornotes}{9/18/26}
The prior 3 pages describes the OLAE, which is machinery to solve the absolute orientation problem, manually.

Surrounding absolute orientation solvers lies the problem of: establishing the correspondence between two point sets of arbitrary dimension.

\begin{equation}
    \pBjB, \ j = 1,2,3,\dots,Q \ \in N_Q \qquad \qquad \pCiC, \ i = 1,2,3,\dots,P \ \in N_P
\end{equation}

Given a measurement $\pCiC \in N_P$ and a model $\pBjB \in N_Q$

then establish randomly an initial minimal correspondence set

\begin{equation}
    \corrSet_1 = \{ (\pCiC, \pBjB) \} \ \in n_1 \, , \qquad n_1 = 3
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \left[ \left( \qCB, \, \pBCC \right)_{1}, \ J_1, \ e_1 \right] = \mathrm{OLAE}(\corrSet_1)
\end{equation}
(orientation solution, cost, residual errors)

consensus set $\rightarrow$ $S_1 = \mathrm{determineConsensusSet}(\corrSet_1, \, \text{residual\_thresh}, \, e_1)$
\end{authornotes}

\noindent\textit{RANSAC exposes exactly three tunable parameters --- the inlier-count threshold $t$, the residual threshold $e$, and a maximum iteration count --- each made explicit in the ``Seed RANSAC'' step below, and returns the consensus set $S_k^*$ upon termination.}
\bigskip

\begin{authornotes}{9/18/26 (cont.)}
One first needs to establish an initial guess of the correspondence set to even seed a RANSAC algorithm.
\begin{itemize}
    \item feature descriptor matching
    \item nearest neighbor \dots
\end{itemize}

Say we do to obtain the set $\corrSet_0 = \{ (\pCiC, \pBjB) \}$, size $n$.

\textbf{Seed RANSAC}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $t$, \# of inliers in the consensus set to stop sampling
    \item $e$, residual errors to consider which belong in the consensus set
    \item max \# of iterations.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{RANSAC}

1: Sample $m$ pts from $\corrSet_0$ \quad ($m$ = minimal \# set for solution)

\begin{equation}
    \qCB, \tB = \mathrm{OLAE}(\corrSet_0)
\end{equation}

$\rightarrow$ transform all $\pBjB$ $\rightarrow$ get residual error for all $n$ points $= \mathrm{res}_i$

\begin{equation}
    S_1^* = \corrSet \{ (\pCiC, \pBjB) \} \text{ that have residual errors} < e
\end{equation}

if \#iter $>$ max\_iter \ OR \ $\mathrm{size}(S_k^*) > t$

\quad return best $S_k^*$

$k$ is RANSAC iterator.
\end{authornotes}

\bigskip
\noindent\textit{The loop above repeats --- resampling a new minimal set from $\corrSet_0$, re-solving via OLAE, and re-evaluating the consensus set --- until one of the two termination conditions above is met, at which point the best consensus set found is returned (and, typically, refined with a final OLAE solve over the full consensus set).}
